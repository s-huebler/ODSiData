Conversely, the kynurenine pathway, mediated by indoleamine
2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), contributes to immunosuppression by
depleting tryptophan and accumulating kynurenines that pro-
mote Treg expansion while impairing effector T cells. Dysbiosis
post-transplant alters tryptophan metabolism, reducing AhR
ligands and exacerbating GVHD.
Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) are also key media-
tors of this pathway, playing an essential role in maintaining
immune tolerance after allo-HSCT. MDSCs express IDO, which
catalyzes tryptophan degradation into immunosuppressive kynur-
enines that activate AhR signaling. This reciprocal interaction
promotes MDSC expansion and enhances their ability to suppress
alloreactive T cell responses, thereby attenuating GvHD severity
while preserving graft-versus-leukemia effects. In addition, MDSCs
release arginase-1 and nitric oxide, further inhibiting T cell
proliferation and supporting Treg induction. Together, these
mechanisms deﬁne the tryptophan–MDSC axis as a central
pathway balancing immune tolerance and inﬂammation in GvHD
[41].
MICROBIOME AND IMMUNE RECONSTITUTION AFTER
ALLOHCT
Immune reconstitution following alloHCT sets the stage for a
successful transplant. A properly reconstituted immune system
requires both innate and adaptive arms for defense against viral
and microbial pathogens, tissue repair, and beneﬁcial graft-vs-
leukemia effects. Immune reconstitution is dependent on factors
including conditioning regimen, graft source and manipulation,
transplant outcomes such as GVHD, and ﬁxed patient factors like
age-related thymic function. For adaptive immunity, numerical T
and B cell recovery typically occurs between 6 to 12 months, while
full reconstitution may take up to 2 years [6, 42]. In parallel, innate
immunity reconstitutes along distinct timelines: NK cells and
subsets of ILCs (ILC1 and ILC3) recover within weeks, whereas ILC2
may take years [43]. ILC, play key role in epithelial tissue repair and
maintain a reciprocal relationship with the microbiome which is
essential for their homeostasis in the gut [44].
Emerging evidence is now revealing that the gut microbiota can
also impact immune recovery post-alloHCT in murine models and
in patients (Table 2). Higher fecal microbial diversity in the early
post-transplant periods strongly associated with improved CD4+
T-cell recovery, particularly in patients receiving CD34-selected
grafts and who rely on de novo lymphopoiesis [45]. In contrast,
low diversity correlates with increased GVHD, infections, and
mortality.
In addition, speciﬁc colonization patterns and microbial nutrient
interactions have been associated with immune deregulation.
Mono-domination, where a single taxon comprises more than
30% of the microbial community, is a common feature during
such periods, particularly during phases like perineutrophil
engraftment [6]. Dietary lactose has been identiﬁed as a key
driver of Enterococcus expansion, with lactose malabsorption
further impairing microbial clearance [46]. Clinically, early dom-
inance of Enterococcus correlates with CD4⁺ lymphopenia and
increased risk of bloodstream infections. In contrast, high
abundance of beneﬁcial commensals such as Blautia has been
associated with accelerated recovery of CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells,
enhanced antiviral immunity, and reduced risk of leukemia relapse
[47]. Moreover, the presence of butyrate-producing taxa—includ-
ing members of Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae—has
been associated with robust B cell reconstitution and intestinal IgA
production, key to mucosal defense [42].
